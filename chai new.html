01 — The problem

Discovering the right molecule is slow, expensive, and mostly trial and error

Materials science usually starts from a molecule that already exists and asks what it's good for. Going the other direction — starting from a property you need and finding a structure that delivers it — traditionally means synthesizing and testing candidate after candidate by hand, in a lab, over months or years.

Most of those candidates fail. The ones that work still need to be discovered before they can be tested, and that discovery step is exactly where a generative model can help: proposing plausible starting points, fast, so the expensive lab time gets spent validating good ideas instead of searching for them.

ChAI doesn't skip the lab — it changes what a chemist starts their week with: a shortlist of structurally sound hypotheses instead of a blank page.

Property → structure, reversed
Classical workflows start from a molecule and measure its properties. Inverse design starts from the property and searches for a structure.
Trial-and-error is the bottleneck
Most synthesized candidates in a discovery campaign don't pan out — the cost is in finding out which ones do.
A narrower shortlist, sooner
A generative first pass can't replace lab validation, but it can narrow where that validation effort goes.
02 — How ChAI works

One prompt, five real steps

This is the actual pipeline running behind the generator below — not a simplified diagram.

01

Describe the property you need

Plain language: a flexible plastic that dissolves in seawater, a foam that absorbs CO₂, a coating that resists heat. No chemistry notation required.

02

ChAI researches real chemistry first Google Search grounding

Before designing anything, the model searches for known molecule classes, functional groups, and research programs already associated with that property.

03

It proposes two designs, not one

A biodegradable-preferred structure that satisfies the request wherever hydrolyzable or compostable chemistry can do the job, plus a non-biodegradable alternative shown for honest comparison — never hidden.

04

Structure, bonds, and a 3D model

Backbone, functional groups, bond types, predicted properties, applications, and a rotatable ball-and-stick model built from the same atom and bond list the AI reasoned about.

05

A scientist validates the best ideas

ChAI's output is a starting hypothesis. Every candidate still needs synthesis and testing in a real lab before it means anything.

04 — The wider field

ChAI is a small, honest demo of a real research direction

Generative and inverse molecular design is an active area across industry and academia. ChAI doesn't compete with these — it demonstrates the same idea at student scale.

Google DeepMind
GNoME

Used graph neural networks to propose millions of candidate inorganic crystal structures, dramatically narrowing the search space for materials worth synthesizing.

IBM Research
Generative chemistry

Explores generative models and reaction-prediction AI (including RXN for Chemistry) that suggest novel molecules and routes to make them.

Insilico Medicine
Generative drug design

Uses generative AI to design candidate drug molecules, with some AI-designed candidates advancing into human clinical trials.

Iambic Therapeutics
Generative + physics models

Combines generative chemistry models with physics-based simulation to propose and rank drug candidates.

Kebotix
Self-driving labs

Pairs generative molecular design with robotic synthesis and testing to close the loop between proposal and validation.

Citrine Informatics
Materials informatics

Applies AI to materials R&D data to help teams prioritize which candidate materials to test next.

What a generative first pass actually buys you

→

Faster exploration

Minutes to see a plausible candidate instead of the first weeks of a discovery campaign.

→

Lower cost, less waste

Fewer blind syntheses — lab time goes toward testing promising structures, not finding them.

Sustainability by default

Wider access

⚠

ChAI does not replace scientists

Every molecule on this page is a hypothesis, not a discovery. Structures, bond lists, and 3D coordinates are AI-generated to be chemically plausible — consistent with valence rules and known bonding patterns — but none of them have been synthesized, measured, or verified in a laboratory.

Predicted properties and applications are conceptual estimates, not measured data. Treat every result as a starting hypothesis that a qualified chemist would still need to evaluate, synthesize, and test before it means anything.

ChAI's job is to help scientists explore promising ideas faster — saving time, cost, and material — not to tell them what's true.
